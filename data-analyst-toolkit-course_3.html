What data actually is

Everything else in this course sits on top of one idea: a dataset is a set of observations about things, arranged so a machine can count them. Get this straight and SQL, pandas and dashboards all become variations on a theme.

⏱️ 15 min 🎯 3 challenges 🏆 1 badge

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Describe any dataset in terms of observations, variables and values
  • Work out the unit of analysis (the "grain") of a table, and say why it matters
  • Tell tidy data from untidy data, and long format from wide
  • Explain what metadata is and why analysts guard it
Lesson

The rectangle

Almost all the data you will ever analyse arrives as a rectangle. Rows going down, columns going across. It looks so ordinary that people skip past it, and then spend years confused about joins and pivots because they never named the parts.

Three words do the work:

  • Observation (or record, or case): one thing you measured. One row.
  • Variable (or field, or attribute): one property you measured about that thing. One column.
  • Value: what that property was, for that thing. One cell.
--  variables ------------------------------>
   respondent_id   age   region      owns_car
1        R0001      34   Scotland    Yes        <- one observation
2        R0002      67   London      No
3        R0003      22   Wales       Yes
                            ^ one value

That is it. A survey response, a Formula 1 pit stop, a card payment, a website visit, a temperature reading from a sensor: all of them squash into rows of observations with columns of variables. The reason a database can answer questions about millions of rows in milliseconds is that it can assume this shape.

If you have worked with survey data: your observation is usually one respondent, and your variables are the questions. The vocabulary here is just borrowed from statistics rather than from research.

Lesson

The unit of analysis, and why it trips people up

The single most useful question you can ask about a table is: what does one row represent? That is the unit of analysis. In database and warehouse work the same idea is called the grain of the table.

It sounds trivial until a table has more than one thing going on. Consider two tables about the same Formula 1 season:

TableOne row is...Grain
driversone driverdriver
resultsone driver in one racedriver per race
pit_stopsone stop by one driver in one racestop per driver per race

Now watch what happens if you ignore that. You want the average age of drivers on the grid. If you calculate it from results rather than drivers, every driver appears once per race, so you have silently weighted each driver by how many races they entered. The number you get is not wrong arithmetic, it is the right answer to a question nobody asked.

The classic symptom: your totals come out suspiciously large, or a count of "customers" is bigger than the number of customers who exist. Nine times out of ten you have joined to a finer grain and are now counting rows, not things.

Whenever you open an unfamiliar table, say out loud what one row is. If you cannot, you are not ready to analyse it yet.

Challenge
Challenge 1 of 3 · Grain check
A table called orders_items has one row per product line within an order. An order can contain several products. You run SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders_items WHERE order_date = '2026-06-01' and report it as "orders placed that day". What have you actually reported?
AThe number of orders, which is correct
BThe number of product lines, which will overstate orders whenever an order has more than one product
CThe number of unique customers
DNothing meaningful, because you cannot count a table like this
Correct: B. The grain is order line, not order. COUNT(*) counts rows, and here a row is a line. To count orders you need COUNT(DISTINCT order_id). This exact mistake, at scale, is how dashboards end up reporting numbers that nobody can reconcile with the finance system.
Lesson

Tidy data, and the long versus wide problem

A rectangle can still be laid out badly. "Tidy" data, a term from Hadley Wickham that you will hear constantly, means three things hold at once:

  • Each variable is a column.
  • Each observation is a row.
  • Each type of observational unit forms its own table.

The most common violation is stuffing values into column names. Here is the same data twice:

-- WIDE: readable for humans, awkward for machines
brand      awareness_2024  awareness_2025  awareness_2026
Kestrel              0.71            0.74            0.76
Vireo                0.83            0.84            0.85

-- LONG (tidy): one row per observation
brand      year   metric      value
Kestrel    2024   awareness    0.71
Kestrel    2025   awareness    0.74
Kestrel    2026   awareness    0.76
Vireo      2024   awareness    0.83

In the wide version, 2024 is a value of a variable called year, but it is masquerading as a column name. Every time you want to add a year you change the shape of the table, and every calculation across years needs bespoke code.

Long format is what tools want. Charting libraries, statistical models, GROUP BY and pandas groupby all expect one row per observation. Wide format is what people want to read. So the working pattern is: store and compute long, present wide. Reshaping between the two is such a routine job that both SQL and pandas have dedicated tools for it, and you will meet them later in this course.

The survey parallel: a cross-tab is wide by design, because a human reads it. The underlying respondent-level file is long-ish. Knowing which shape you are in, and which shape the next step needs, is most of the skill.

Data about the data

Metadata is everything that tells you how to read the values. Without it, a column of numbers is just numbers.

KindWhat it capturesWhere you meet it
StructuralColumn names, data types, keys, table relationshipsThe database schema
DescriptiveWhat a variable means, question wording, units, value labelsData dictionary, codebook
AdministrativeWho collected it, when, how, what licence, retention rulesDocumentation, governance tooling

Consider the value 3 in a column called satisfaction. Is that "quite satisfied" on a 5 point scale, or "very dissatisfied" on a scale that runs the other way? Is 9 a valid response or a code for "don't know"? Metadata is what stops you presenting nonsense with total confidence.

A data dictionary is the artefact that carries this. Column name, description, type, permitted values, and derivation if it is a calculated field. When you join a new team, asking "is there a data dictionary?" is a strong first question, and the answer tells you a great deal about the organisation.

You already own this problem: in R, labelled survey data carries value labels and variable labels alongside the numbers. That is descriptive metadata riding along with the values, and it is why stripping labels carelessly turns a meaningful file into anonymous integers.

Interview note
💡 Interview note

Grain is the question behind the question

"Here are two tables. How would you work out the average order value?"

Interviewers use questions like this to see whether you check the grain before you calculate. The answer they want to hear starts with a question of your own: what does one row represent in each table? Then: is order value stored once per order, or repeated on every line of the order?

Saying "before I write anything, I would confirm the grain of each table and whether the measure repeats" marks you out immediately, because it is exactly the habit that prevents the most expensive category of reporting error. Candidates who dive straight into SUM() and AVG() get the arithmetic right and the answer wrong.

Challenge
Challenge 3 of 3 · Reading a schema cold
You are handed a table with columns store_id, product_id, week, units_sold. What is the unit of analysis?
AOne store
BOne product
COne product, in one store, in one week
DOne sale
Correct: C. The grain is the combination of the identifying columns, here store, product and week together. Those three columns form a composite key. Notice it is not one sale: individual sales have been aggregated into weekly totals already, which is why the measure is units_sold rather than a row per transaction. Spotting that the data has been pre-aggregated matters, because you can go coarser from here but you can never get back to individual transactions.

Recap

What to take away

Show the 5 takeaways5▾
  • A dataset is observations (rows) described by variables (columns) holding values (cells)
  • The unit of analysis, or grain, is what one row represents, and it decides whether your aggregates mean anything
  • Counting rows is not the same as counting things: reach for COUNT(DISTINCT ...) when the grain is finer than the thing you want
  • Tidy data means one variable per column and one observation per row: compute long, present wide
  • Metadata carries meaning, and a data dictionary is where it should live

Where data comes from

Data does not exist. It is made, by someone, for some purpose, using some method. Every one of those choices leaves fingerprints on the numbers you end up analysing, and knowing how to read those fingerprints is what separates an analyst from a calculator.

  • Distinguish declared from observed data, and know when each one lies
  • Use the first, second and third party framing that commercial teams use

Lesson

Two ways of knowing anything

Strip away the jargon and there are only two ways to find out about people or systems. You can ask, or you can watch.

Declared data (asking)Observed data (watching)
ExamplesSurveys, interviews, focus groups, reviews, NPSClickstream, transactions, till scans, sensors, server logs
Good atWhy. Attitudes, motivations, intent, awareness, brand perceptionWhat. Actual behaviour, at scale, continuously
Bad atAccuracy of recall, socially awkward truths, predicting behaviourExplaining motive, capturing anything outside the system
Fails throughRecall error, social desirability, question wording, who respondsTracking gaps, bot traffic, people sharing devices, definition drift

The failure modes are the interesting part. Ask people how much exercise they do and the average will be flattering, because people round their self-image upwards. Watch their phone accelerometer and you get the truth about movement, but you learn nothing about why they stopped going to the gym.

Serious analysis usually needs both. Behavioural data tells you a segment of customers stopped buying in March. Only declared data tells you it was because a competitor undercut you and the delivery experience annoyed them.

Why you have an advantage here: most people arriving at data analysis from a computing route have only ever handled observed data, and they tend to treat it as simply true. You already know that a number is a measurement produced by a method. That scepticism is a real professional asset, so do not leave it behind.

In practice, data arrives in recognisable families. You will meet all of these.

Two distinctions cut across all of them. Primary data was collected for your specific question; secondary data was collected for someone else's and you are repurposing it. And the commercial framing you will hear in any marketing or product meeting:

  • First party: collected by your organisation, from your own customers or users. Most trusted, most limited in reach.
  • Second party: another organisation's first party data, shared with you through a partnership.
  • Third party: aggregated and sold on by an intermediary. Broadest reach, weakest provenance, and the category most disrupted by privacy regulation and the decline of tracking cookies.

The operational data trap

One family deserves its own warning, because it catches nearly everyone once.

Operational systems are built to run a business, not to remember its history. A CRM record for a customer holds their current address, current plan, current status. When something changes, the old value is usually overwritten and gone.

So if you ask "how many customers were on the premium plan last March?" and you answer it by filtering today's customer table, you get a nonsense number: everyone who is premium now and happened to exist then. Customers who downgraded in between have quietly reclassified themselves in your history.

The name for the fix: a warehouse solves this with a slowly changing dimension, keeping one row per version of the customer with valid-from and valid-to dates. If you need history, you need either that or a periodic snapshot. You cannot reconstruct it after the fact from a table that overwrites.

Practical rule: before answering any "as at some past date" question, establish whether the source stores history at all. It is a two minute conversation that saves a fortnight of wrong reporting.

Interview note
💡 Interview note

"What would you check before trusting this dataset?"

Also asked as: "You have been given a file and asked to report on it. What do you do first?"

This is a favourite because it separates people who interrogate data from people who process it. A strong answer runs through provenance rather than statistics:

Where did it come from and who collected it. Why was it collected, since a source built for billing will not answer a marketing question cleanly. What is the grain, and does it match my question. What population does it cover, and who is missing. How current is it, and how often does it refresh. What are the definitions of the key fields, and have they changed over the period. Does it store history or only current state.

Finish with the sentence that gets you hired: "I would reconcile a couple of totals against a source the business already trusts before I show anyone anything."

Types and levels of measurement

There is a rule that quietly governs every chart and every statistic you will ever produce: what you are allowed to do with a number depends on what kind of number it is. Nearly every embarrassing analytical mistake is a violation of that rule.

⏱️ 15 min 🎯 3 challenges 🏆 1 badge

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Separate how data is stored (data types) from what it means (measurement levels)
  • Place any variable on the nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio ladder
  • Decide which summary statistics and charts a variable can legitimately support
  • Recognise the mean-of-a-Likert-scale argument and take a defensible position on it
Lesson

Storage type is not meaning

Two different questions get muddled constantly, so let us separate them at the outset.

Data type is how the computer stores a value: integer, float, text, boolean, date, timestamp. It governs what operations will not crash.

Level of measurement is what the value means: whether the numbers represent categories, an order, or a genuine quantity. It governs what operations are not nonsense.

A postcode district stored as text and a region stored as the integers 1 to 12 are both categories. The integers are only labels wearing a numeric costume. Nothing stops you calculating the mean region, and the answer, 6.5, refers to no place on earth.

# Perfectly legal. Completely meaningless.
df["region_code"].mean()   # -> 6.5

The asymmetry to remember: the computer protects you from type errors and never protects you from measurement errors. Nothing will warn you. This is why the level of measurement has to live in your head, or in your data dictionary.

Lesson

The four levels

The ladder runs from least to most informative. Each rung permits everything the rungs below permit, plus something new.

LevelWhat it supportsExamplesLegitimate summary
NominalCategories only, no orderBrand, region, gender, statusCount, mode, proportion
OrdinalOrder, but unequal or unknown gapsLikert agreement, social grade, race finishing position, education levelMedian, quartiles, and the above
IntervalEqual gaps, but an arbitrary zeroTemperature in Celsius, calendar years, standardised scoresMean, standard deviation, differences
RatioEqual gaps and a true zeroRevenue, age, distance, duration, countsEverything, including ratios and percentage change

The interval and ratio distinction sounds academic until you try to divide. 20°C is not "twice as warm" as 10°C, because zero Celsius is a convention rather than an absence of heat. Convert both to Kelvin, which has a true zero, and the ratio changes entirely. Meanwhile £20 is twice £10, because zero pounds is genuinely no money. Ratios need a real zero.

Cutting across the ladder: discrete variables take separated values you can count (number of children, races entered), continuous variables can take any value in a range (weight, lap time). It matters mostly for charting: discrete counts suit bars, continuous distributions suit histograms.

The Likert argument, and how to survive it

Here is a live disagreement you will encounter, and being able to argue both sides of it is a genuine mark of competence.

You have a 5 point agreement scale: strongly disagree to strongly agree, coded 1 to 5. Are you allowed to report the mean?

The strict view: no. The scale is ordinal. You know 4 outranks 3, but you do not know that the distance from "neither" to "agree" equals the distance from "agree" to "strongly agree". Averaging assumes equal spacing you have not earned. Report the median, or report the distribution.

The pragmatic view: in practice, means of multi-point scales behave well, are more sensitive to small shifts than medians, and are what stakeholders understand. Treating a 5 or 7 point scale as approximately interval is standard across academic psychology and commercial research alike. Adding several scale items into a composite index strengthens the case further.

What experienced analysts actually do: report the distribution as the primary finding, usually as a top-two-box percentage, and use the mean as a supporting sensitivity measure for tracking movement over time. That way the headline never depends on a contested assumption.

Top-two-box collapses "agree" and "strongly agree" into a single percentage. It is ordinal-safe, robust, and immediately legible to a non-technical reader. It is also lossy, since it hides whether people shifted from strongly agree to agree, which is exactly the movement a mean would catch. Reporting both covers you.

Interview note
💡 Interview note

"Can you take the mean of a Likert scale?"

A deliberately unfair question. There is no clean right answer, and that is the point.

Anyone who answers with a flat yes or a flat no has told the interviewer they do not know it is contested. The answer that lands: "Strictly it is ordinal, so the mean assumes equal spacing that the scale does not guarantee. In practice it is widely done and usually behaves well. I would lead with the distribution or a top-two-box figure, and use the mean to track movement, so the headline does not rest on the assumption."

The same structure works for any "is this allowed" question in analysis: name the technical objection, acknowledge the practical convention, then state what you would actually do and why. It demonstrates the thing they are really testing, which is judgement under ambiguity rather than recall.

The question before the query

The hardest part of an analyst's job is not writing the query. It is working out which query would settle the matter. Analysts who cannot do this produce technically flawless answers to questions nobody needed answering.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Turn a vague business request into an answerable analytical question
  • Define a metric precisely enough that two people would calculate it identically
  • Place a request on the descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, prescriptive ladder
  • Run the "so what" test before you present anything
Lesson

Requests are not questions

Stakeholders rarely ask you an answerable question. They ask something like:

"Can you send me the sales numbers?"
"Is the campaign working?"
"Why are customers leaving?"
"Can we get a dashboard for this?"

None of these is answerable as stated. Each is a symptom of an underlying decision the person needs to make, and your first job is to find that decision. The translation runs in three steps.

1. The business question. What decision hangs on this? "Is the campaign working?" usually means "should we keep funding it, cut it, or change it?" That reframing tells you the answer needs to support a spending decision, so it needs a comparison and a cost.

2. The analytical question. Now make it precise and measurable: "Did customers exposed to the June campaign purchase at a higher rate within 30 days than comparable unexposed customers, and is the gap larger than the campaign cost per acquisition?"

3. The data question. Only now: which tables, which fields, which filters, which grain. This is the part you were probably itching to start with.

The single most useful question to ask a stakeholder: "What would you do differently depending on the answer?" If they cannot answer, either the analysis is not needed, or you have not found the real question yet. Both are worth knowing before you spend three days on it.

Defining a metric so it survives contact

"Active users" sounds unambiguous. It is not. Consider the decisions hiding inside it:

  • Active doing what? Logged in, or performed a meaningful action?
  • Over what window? Daily, weekly, rolling 28 days?
  • Counting what? Accounts, devices, or people (who may hold several accounts)?
  • Including whom? Internal staff, test accounts, bots, free trials?
  • Attributed when? The date of the action, or the date the record landed in the warehouse?

Every one of those choices changes the number, sometimes by a lot. Two analysts given the same brief and the same database will produce different figures, both defensible, and the meeting will be spent arguing about arithmetic instead of the decision.

A usable metric definition therefore states five things: the numerator, the denominator if there is one, the time window, the inclusions and exclusions, and the grain at which it is calculated.

Metric: 28 day active customer rate
Numerator   distinct customer_id with >=1 completed order
Denominator distinct customer_id with an active account on the last day
Window      the 28 days ending on the reporting date, inclusive
Exclusions  staff accounts, test orders, orders later fully refunded
Grain       reported per market, per week

Watch the refund clause. That single exclusion means the number for a given week can change after the fact, as refunds land. A metric that revises itself is fine, provided everyone knows. Undocumented, it looks like your reporting is broken.

The "so what" test

Before anything leaves your hands, run it through three questions.

So what? If the reader accepts this finding completely, what changes? A finding with no consequence is trivia, however hard it was to compute.

Compared to what? A number alone means nothing. "Awareness is 34%" is not a finding. Against last quarter, against the competitor, against the target, against the other market: now it is a finding. Every number needs a comparator, and choosing the comparator is an analytical act, not a formatting one.

How sure am I? Is this movement bigger than the noise? Would it survive a different but equally reasonable set of choices about definitions and filters? If a two percentage point shift reverses when you handle refunds differently, you do not have a finding, you have an artefact.

A survey background gives you a head start on "compared to what". Longitudinal survey work is built on comparison: wave on wave, brand versus competitor set, market versus market. That instinct for a comparator is exactly what many analysts lack, and it is why they produce dashboards full of context-free numbers.

Samples, bias and uncertainty

Your data is almost never everybody. Understanding who is missing, and how confident you are entitled to be, is the part of the job that protects your employer from expensive mistakes and protects you from making them.

  • Name the main biases by mechanism, not just by label

Lesson

Population, frame, sample

Three nested ideas, and the gaps between them are where bias lives.

  • Population: everyone or everything you want to draw conclusions about. "UK adults who own a smartphone."
  • Sampling frame: the list you can actually reach. An online panel, a customer database, everyone who has an account.
  • Sample: the ones who ended up in your data. Those who were invited, qualified, and finished.

Two gaps open up. Coverage error is population minus frame: people you wanted to study but could never have reached. If your frame is an online panel, UK adults with no internet access are invisible to you, no matter how many interviews you conduct. Non-response is frame minus sample: people you could have reached who did not take part, and who differ systematically from those who did.

The point people miss: increasing sample size shrinks random error and does nothing whatsoever to coverage or non-response bias. A million responses from an unrepresentative frame gives you a very precise estimate of the wrong quantity. Bias does not average out.

Behavioural data has exactly the same problem in different clothing. Your event data covers users of your app, on supported devices, who accepted tracking consent. That is your frame. It is not the market.

How sure are you allowed to be?

Every estimate from a sample carries uncertainty. Three ideas cover most of what you need.

Margin of error. For a simple random sample, a percentage near 50% has a 95% margin of error of roughly 1 / √n. For n = 1000 that is about ±3 points. So 52% means "probably somewhere between 49% and 55%", and a rise from 49% to 52% between waves is well inside the noise.

Rough 95% margin of error, percentages near 50%
n =   100   ->  ±10 points
n =   400   ->  ±5 points
n = 1,000   ->  ±3 points
n = 4,000   ->  ±1.5 points

Note the shape: quartering the error costs sixteen times the sample.

Statistical significance. A test asks: if there were genuinely no difference, how often would I see a gap this large by chance alone? A p-value below 0.05 conventionally means "rarely enough that I will treat it as real". Two things to hold onto: significance is not importance (with a huge sample, a trivial difference becomes significant), and testing many comparisons guarantees false positives. Test twenty subgroups at 95% confidence and one will light up by chance. This is why fishing through a cross-tab for anything with a star on it is not analysis.

Effect size. How big is the difference, in units anyone cares about? Report this alongside significance, always. "Significantly higher" without a magnitude is an incomplete sentence.

Confidence intervals, stated carefully: a 95% interval means that if you repeated the whole sampling procedure many times, about 95% of the intervals produced would contain the true value. It does not mean there is a 95% probability that this particular interval contains it. Interviewers do occasionally probe this, and more importantly the loose version encourages overconfidence.

Correlation, causation, and the confounder

Everyone can recite that correlation is not causation. Rather fewer can explain why, which is what actually gets asked.

Two variables move together for four possible reasons. A causes B. B causes A (reverse causation: do loyalty scheme members spend more because of the scheme, or do heavy spenders join schemes?). Something else causes both (a confounder). Or chance, especially if you went looking through many pairs.

The confounder is the interesting case. Ice cream sales correlate with drowning deaths. Nothing causal connects them; hot weather raises both. Confounders are common, often unmeasured, and the reason observational data supports causal claims so weakly.

Simpson's paradox is the confounder's most alarming trick: a relationship can reverse when you account for a grouping variable.

Treatment success rates
                Treatment A      Treatment B
Mild cases      93%  (81/87)     87%  (234/270)
Severe cases    73%  (192/263)   69%  (55/80)
--------------------------------------------------
Overall         78%  (273/350)   83%  (289/350)

A wins in both groups, and loses overall, because A was
given mostly to severe cases and B mostly to mild ones.

Both tables are arithmetically correct. Which one answers the question depends on whether case severity is a confounder, and that is a judgement about the world, not about the data. No amount of computation resolves it.

What does license a causal claim: a randomised experiment, because randomisation balances confounders you did not even think to measure. Failing that, quasi-experimental designs (difference in differences, regression discontinuity, matched controls) which make the assumptions explicit and testable. When someone asks you to prove causation from historical data alone, the professional answer names what would be needed.

Lesson

The six dimensions

Data quality has an agreed vocabulary. Knowing it lets you tell a stakeholder precisely what is wrong instead of "the data is messy".

DimensionQuestionExample failure
CompletenessIs anything missing?40% of records have no region
ValidityDoes it conform to the rules?Ages of 0 and 214; a date of 30 February
AccuracyDoes it match reality?Address is well-formed and the customer moved two years ago
ConsistencyDoes it agree across sources?The CRM and the billing system disagree on account status
UniquenessIs each thing recorded once?The same customer under three email addresses
TimelinessIs it current enough to use?Yesterday's stock levels driving today's ordering

Validity and accuracy are the pair worth separating carefully, because they fail differently. Validity is checkable by machine: you can write a rule that rejects an age of 214. Accuracy usually cannot be checked without an external reference. A plausible, well-formatted, entirely wrong value passes every automated check you can write. This is why validation catches the noisy errors and leaves the dangerous ones.

Profiling first, always. Before analysing anything: row count, distinct count and null count per column, min and max on every numeric and date field, and the frequency of every categorical value. It takes minutes and it catches the majority of problems. In pandas that is info(), describe() and value_counts(), which you will meet in module 10.

Lesson

Missing data is a finding

The instinct is to remove or fill missing values and move on. That instinct is wrong, because the pattern of missingness is often the most informative thing in the file. Statisticians separate three mechanisms.

MCAR, missing completely at random. The missingness has nothing to do with anything. A sensor dropped packets at random intervals. Rare in practice, and the only case where deleting incomplete rows is basically harmless.

MAR, missing at random. The missingness depends on other variables you did observe. Older respondents skipped the income question more often, and you know their age. Recoverable, because you can model or weight using age.

MNAR, missing not at random. The missingness depends on the missing value itself. High earners declined to state their income. This is the dangerous one: your observed incomes are systematically unrepresentative, and no amount of clever imputation from the observed data can fix it, because the information you need was never collected.

Why filling with the mean is usually wrong: it shrinks the variance, weakens correlations, invents certainty that does not exist, and makes it impossible to see afterwards which values were real. If you must impute, prefer a method that reflects uncertainty, and always keep a flag column marking what was imputed.

Before you touch missing values, plot them. Are they concentrated in one date range (a tracking outage), one source system, one market, one segment? A block of nulls starting on a specific Tuesday is not a statistical problem, it is an engineering incident, and the fix is upstream.

Duplicates, outliers and drift

Three more recurring problems, each with a wrong instinctive fix.

Duplicates. Ask first whether the row is genuinely duplicated or whether your grain is finer than you assumed. A customer appearing twice in an orders table is not a duplicate, it is two orders. True duplicates come from re-ingested files, retried API calls, and the same person entered twice under different spellings. Before deduplicating, decide which record wins: the most recent, the most complete, or the one from the more trusted system. Deduplicating blindly on all columns misses the near-duplicates that matter most.

Outliers. Never delete an outlier because it is inconvenient. Investigate it. There are three kinds: an error (a decimal point in the wrong place, a test transaction of £999,999), a legitimate extreme (one genuine wholesale customer among retail buyers), and a different population (a bot in your human traffic). Only the first is a data problem. The second is real and often important. The third means your population definition needs fixing, not your rows.

The medians and means point: if a handful of legitimate extremes dominate your mean, the answer is usually to report the median as well, or to segment the extremes out and report them separately, rather than to delete them. Deleting real data to make a summary statistic behave is quietly falsifying the record.

Definition drift. The most insidious of the three, because nothing looks broken. A field's meaning changes over time: a question was reworded in wave 6, "active" was redefined in March, a new tracking library started counting a page view differently. Your time series now compares two different things and shows a step change that is pure artefact. Whenever you see a sharp discontinuity in a trend, your first hypothesis should be a definition or instrumentation change, not a change in the world.

Reproducibility is a quality control

An analysis you cannot rerun is an analysis you cannot defend. When someone asks in three months why the number differs from the one you gave in June, "I remember doing some cleaning in Excel" ends the conversation badly.

The standard is straightforward and non-negotiable in a serious team:

  • The raw data is never edited. It is read, never written. Every change happens in code, downstream.
  • Every step is in a script, in order, runnable from a clean start.
  • Code is version controlled, so the state that produced any past number can be recovered.
  • Assumptions are written down next to the code that implements them.
  • Outputs are regenerated, not patched. If a number is wrong, fix the script and rerun.

There is a self-interested case here too. The second time a stakeholder asks for the same thing with one filter changed, reproducibility is the difference between two minutes and two days.

The manual-fix temptation is strongest under deadline. Nudging a value in the output because the deadline is in an hour feels pragmatic and creates a number that exists nowhere in your code. When someone reconciles it later, and they will, you cannot explain it. If you must patch, patch in the script with a dated comment saying why.

Why you are not querying the live system

Databases come in two temperaments, built for opposite jobs.

OLTP (transactional)OLAP (analytical)

The row versus column distinction explains a lot. A transactional store keeps each record's fields physically together, so fetching one customer is a single quick read. When you ask for one column out of forty, a columnar store does roughly one fortieth of the work.

This is also why analysts are handed a copy of the data rather than access to production. Your innocent full-table scan can lock tables and slow the live application, so organisations replicate data into an analytical environment where you can be as expensive as you like.

Practical consequence for your SQL: on a columnar warehouse, SELECT * is genuinely wasteful in a way it is not on a small transactional database, because it forces the engine to read every column you did not need. Naming your columns is a performance habit, not just a tidiness one.

Reading a star schema

Warehouse tables are usually arranged in a pattern called a star schema, and once you can see it, unfamiliar warehouses stop being intimidating.

Fact tables hold the events and the measures. Long, narrow, constantly growing. One row per thing that happened, plus numeric columns you can add up, plus ID columns pointing outwards.

Dimension tables hold the descriptive context. Short, wide, relatively static. One row per customer, per product, per store, per date, with all the attributes you want to slice and filter by.

              dim_date              dim_product
                  |                      |
                  |                      |
  dim_store ---  fact_sales  --- dim_customer
                     |
        (store_id, product_id, date_id, customer_id,
         units, gross_value, discount, net_value)

Analysis then has a standard shape: aggregate a measure from the fact table, sliced by attributes from the dimensions. "Net value by product category by month" means joining fact_sales to dim_product and dim_date, grouping, summing. Once you recognise this, most reporting SQL writes itself.

Two pieces of vocabulary that come with it:

  • A primary key uniquely identifies a row in its own table. customer_id in dim_customer.
  • A foreign key is that same identifier appearing in another table, pointing back. customer_id in fact_sales.

And the concept from module 1 returns, because it is the single most important property of a fact table: its grain. One row per order line, or per order, or per day per store? Everything about how you aggregate depends on the answer, and it is the first thing to establish.

The fan trap. Join two fact tables of different grain (orders and shipments, say) and rows multiply: each order matched against each of its shipments. Now SUM(order_value) counts the same order value several times. The symptom is a total that is too large by an unpredictable factor. The fix is to aggregate each fact table to a common grain before joining them, which is exactly what CTEs are for.

Lesson

ETL, ELT and the question about freshness

Data has to get from source systems into your warehouse. Two orderings of the same three jobs:

  • ETL: extract, transform, then load. Clean it on the way in, so the warehouse holds only modelled data. Traditional, tidy, and you cannot recover detail that was discarded during transformation.
  • ELT: extract, load, then transform. Dump the raw data into cheap cloud storage first, transform it inside the warehouse afterwards. Now dominant, because storage is cheap and warehouse compute is powerful. It means the raw data survives, so you can rebuild your models when definitions change.

Both run on a schedule, and that schedule is the source of the most common misunderstanding between analysts and stakeholders. A dashboard showing yesterday evening's data at 9am today is working exactly as designed, and looks broken to someone expecting live figures.

Ask three questions about any table you rely on: how often does it refresh, what time did it last succeed, and what happens to the numbers if a source is late or a run fails? Silent pipeline failures that leave yesterday's data in place are far more damaging than loud ones, because the dashboard still looks fine.

Related vocabulary you will hear: an orchestrator (Airflow, Dagster, Prefect) schedules the steps and handles failures; idempotent means a job produces the same result if it is rerun, which is what makes recovery from failure safe; backfill means rerunning history after fixing a bug or changing a definition.

Governance: production holds personal data that analysts should not have wholesale access to, and a warehouse layer is where access control, pseudonymisation and audit sit.

From finding to insight

A finding is something true about the data. An insight is something true about the business that somebody can act on. The gap between them is where analysts either earn their reputation or quietly fail to.

⏱️ 15 min 🎯 3 challenges 🏆 1 badge

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Tell observations, findings, insights and recommendations apart
  • Structure an analysis so the answer arrives first
  • Use benchmarks, indices and decomposition to give numbers meaning

Lesson

Four rungs, and where most work stops

Watch the same piece of analysis climb.

RungExampleValue
Observation"Sales in the North were £2.1m in Q2."None on its own
Finding"Northern sales fell 8% against Q1, while other regions grew 3%."Now there is a comparator, so there is something to explain
Insight"The fall is entirely in the value range, and concentrated in stores within 3 miles of a competitor that cut prices in April. Full-price lines held up."Now it is explained and localised, so it can be acted on
Recommendation"Match on the twelve value lines in the 40 affected stores, at an estimated margin cost of £180k against £600k of exposed revenue."Now it is a decision, with a cost attached

Most reporting stops at rung one and calls it a dashboard. Most competent analysts reach rung two. Reaching rung three consistently is what gets you promoted, and it requires exactly one habit: never stop at the first number that answers the question. Always ask where it concentrates.

That habit has a technique behind it: decomposition. An aggregate number is always the product or sum of smaller ones, and the story is nearly always in one component rather than spread evenly.

Revenue = customers × orders per customer × items per order × price per item

Revenue fell 6%. Which term moved? If customers held steady
and orders per customer fell, that is a retention story.
If price per item fell, that is a discounting story.
Same 6%, completely different meeting.

Then split by dimension: region, segment, channel, product, tenure, device. You are looking for concentration. A change spread evenly across everything usually means something systemic (price, season, measurement); a change concentrated in one slice usually has a specific, findable cause.

Lesson

Giving a number meaning

Three standard devices for making a number interpretable.

Benchmarks. Against what? Previous period, same period last year (which controls for seasonality), target, competitor, category average, or the rest of the business. Each answers a different question, and picking the wrong comparator is a quiet way to mislead. Comparing December against November in retail tells you almost nothing except that Christmas exists.

Indexing. Rebase a series so a chosen point equals 100, and different-sized things become comparable. If brand A is at 100 and brand B at 137, B is 37% higher on that measure regardless of absolute scale. Indexing is also how you compare a subgroup against the whole: an index of 145 for a segment means it is 45% over-represented. Two cautions: an index hides the base size, so a spectacular index on 30 people is noise; and it hides direction of absolute change, since everything can be falling while one thing indexes above the rest.

Rates and normalisation. Absolute counts mislead whenever the population differs. London always has the most of everything because London has the most people. Per capita, per store, per thousand sessions, per customer: divide by exposure and the comparison becomes fair.

Percentages of percentages. "Conversion rose 2%" is ambiguous and gets misread constantly. Did it go from 4% to 6% (two percentage points, a 50% relative increase) or from 4% to 4.08% (a 2% relative increase)? Use "percentage points" for the difference and "%" for the relative change, and say which you mean. This exact ambiguity has caused real strategic errors.

Lesson

Structure the answer, not the journey

The instinct when presenting is to walk people through the work in the order you did it: here is the data, here is my method, here is what I found, so here is my conclusion. This is the wrong order for every audience except a fellow analyst reviewing your method.

Use the top-down structure instead. Answer first, then support it.

SCQA is the standard frame and it is worth internalising:

  • Situation: the shared context nobody will dispute. "Northern sales have grown every quarter for two years."
  • Complication: what changed. "In Q2 they fell 8%, while other regions grew."
  • Question: the question that raises. "What caused it, and is it structural?"
  • Answer: your finding, stated up front, with the support underneath.

Two practical rules follow. Give every slide a title that states the finding, not the topic: "Value lines drove the Northern decline" rather than "Northern performance by category". A deck whose titles read as a sequence of sentences carries the argument even if nobody reads the charts. And put the method in an appendix, available to anyone who asks, absent from the main line of argument.

This is where survey experience transfers directly. Debriefing a survey wave is exactly this discipline: what moved, for whom, why, and what should be done about it. Many analysts arriving from an engineering background have never had to stand in front of a stakeholder and defend a story, and it shows. Lead with it.

Interview note
💡 Interview note

"Tell me about an analysis that changed a decision."

This is the question most candidates answer badly, by describing technique. The interviewer does not want your method, they want evidence that your work had consequences. Use four beats and keep it under two minutes:

Showing data: charts that work

A chart is an argument made with position, length and colour. Choosing the wrong encoding does not just look bad, it makes a claim the data cannot support, and it is one of the few analytical mistakes your whole audience can see.

⏱️ 17 min 🎯 3 challenges 🏆 1 badge

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Choose a chart from the task, not from the chart menu
  • Rank visual encodings by how accurately people read them
  • Spot the standard distortions: truncated axes, dual axes, bad colour
  • Design for the reader, including readers who cannot distinguish red from green
Lesson

Encodings, ranked by accuracy

A chart turns numbers into visual properties. People read some of those properties far more accurately than others, and the ranking is well established by experiment:

  1. Position along a common scale (bars from a shared baseline, points on the same axis). Most accurate by a wide margin.
  2. Position on identical but separate scales (small multiples).
  3. Length without a common baseline (stacked bar segments above the first).
  4. Angle and slope (pie slices, unlabelled line gradients).
  5. Area (bubbles, treemaps). People systematically underestimate area differences.
  6. Colour intensity and saturation (heatmaps, choropleths). Fine for pattern, poor for value.
  7. Volume, and anything in 3D. Essentially decorative.

This ranking is the entire justification for the advice you have heard. Bar charts beat pie charts because position on a common scale beats angle. Never use 3D bars because you have taken an accurate encoding and added an inaccurate one on top. Bubble charts are for showing that something is bigger, not how much bigger.

It also explains the one legitimate case for a pie chart: two or three parts of a clearly-labelled whole, where the point is "roughly half" rather than a precise comparison. Beyond about four slices, nobody can rank them.

Stacked bars, the useful nuance: the bottom segment sits on a common baseline so it reads accurately. Every segment above it floats, so comparing those across bars is genuinely hard. Put the series you most want compared at the bottom, and if every series matters equally, use grouped bars or small multiples instead.

Do not start from "which chart shall I use?" Start from "what am I asking the reader to do?" There are five recurring tasks.

TaskReader questionChart

Change over timeWhich way is it going?Line chart. Bars only for few, discrete periods
CompositionWhat is it made of?Stacked bar; 100% stacked for share; pie only for 2 or 3 parts
DistributionHow is it spread?Histogram, box plot, or a strip plot if the group is small
RelationshipDo these move together?Scatter plot, with a trend line if it is justified

Two additions worth knowing. Small multiples: repeat the same small chart once per category, on identical axes. It is the most underused chart in business reporting and it beats a single overcrowded chart almost every time, because comparing across panels with the same scale is easy while untangling twelve lines is not. And slope charts for before-and-after across many categories: two vertical axes, one line per category. Change of rank becomes immediately visible.

The survey instinct to resist: the reflex to put every brand and every wave on one chart because the data exists. A chart with twelve series communicates that the data is complicated. Small multiples, or one chart per question with the comparison you actually want to make, communicates the finding.

Lesson

How charts mislead

Four distortions account for most misleading business charts. Three are usually accidental.

The truncated axis. A bar chart whose y-axis starts at 90 instead of 0 turns a 3 point difference into a visual doubling. Because bars encode value as length, cutting the baseline breaks the encoding: the bar is no longer proportional to the number. Bars must start at zero. Lines are different, because a line encodes change rather than magnitude, so a sensible non-zero range is legitimate and often necessary. Just make the range obvious.

Dual axes. Two series on two different y-axes, scaled independently. The apparent relationship between them is entirely a product of where you chose to put the axes, and you can manufacture or destroy a "correlation" by rescaling. Almost always better: index both to 100 at a common starting point and use one axis, or use two stacked panels sharing an x-axis.

Colour carrying no information. Giving each bar its own colour when the categories are already labelled adds visual noise and implies grouping that does not exist. Colour should encode something: a category the reader must track across charts, a highlight on the one series that matters, or a scale for a continuous value. Everything else is grey.

Missing base sizes. A percentage without an n is unverifiable. A 62% that rests on 21 respondents deserves different treatment from one resting on 2,100, and only the footnote tells the reader which they are looking at.

Accessibility is not optional. Roughly 1 in 12 men has some form of colour vision deficiency, most commonly red and green. A red-versus-green "bad versus good" chart is unreadable to them. Use blue and orange, vary lightness as well as hue, and label directly on the chart rather than relying on a legend. This turns up in interviews for anyone who will build dashboards.

Lesson

Designing a dashboard that gets used

Most dashboards fail the same way: they display everything available rather than answering something specific. A few principles fix the majority of it.

Start from the question, not the data. Write the question at the top of the page, literally. "Are we on track this month?" Every element either helps answer it or comes off.

One primary question per page. If there are three questions, that is three pages or three tabs, not one crowded canvas.

Establish a visual hierarchy. The headline number top left, where eyes land first. Trend next to it, so the number has direction. Breakdowns below. Detail tables last, or behind a click.

Every number gets a comparator. A KPI tile showing "£1.2m" is nearly useless. "£1.2m, 8% ahead of last month, 3% behind target" is a status.

Annotate the anomalies. A text note saying "step change from tracking upgrade on 14 March" saves a dozen confused questions and stops people quietly disbelieving the chart.

State freshness on the page. "Data to 23 July, refreshed 06:00 daily." Absent that, every unexpected number becomes "is this even up to date?"

Filter defaults matter more than filter options. Most people never change a filter. What the page shows on load is what the organisation will believe.

The five second test: show the dashboard to someone for five seconds, take it away, and ask what the headline was. If they cannot say, the hierarchy has failed. This works just as well on a slide.

Self-serve, single source of truth, and why numbers disagree

Every organisation of any size eventually hits the same crisis: three teams present three different figures for the same metric in the same meeting. Everyone is querying honestly. Everyone is right by their own definition.

The causes are always among these: different definitions of the metric, different date logic (order date, dispatch date, or the date the record landed), different exclusions (test accounts, refunds, internal users), different refresh times, or different source tables that were built for different purposes.

The structural answer is a single source of truth: one governed set of modelled tables, with metric definitions written once in a semantic layer that every dashboard and query reads from. Change the definition of "active customer" in one place and it changes everywhere, consistently. This is why analytics engineering exists as a discipline, and why you will hear about tools such as dbt, whose real contribution is putting business logic into version-controlled, tested, documented code.

Self-serve is the other half. The dream is that stakeholders answer their own routine questions and analysts do the hard work. It succeeds only when the underlying data is modelled, the definitions are certified and the fields are named for humans rather than for the source system. Given raw tables and a drag-and-drop tool, people confidently produce wrong numbers, and you spend your week explaining why two dashboards disagree.

The version of this you may already know: a table specification exists so that everyone agrees what a base is, what is netted with what, and which respondents are excluded. A semantic layer is the same instinct applied to a whole business, in code rather than in a document.

Interview note
💡 Interview note

"Two teams report different numbers for the same metric. How do you resolve it?"

A senior-sounding question that many candidates answer as a technical debugging exercise. It is partly a diplomacy question.

The method: get both queries side by side rather than arguing about the outputs. Then compare, in this order, definition (numerator, denominator, inclusions, exclusions), date logic (which date field, which timezone, inclusive or exclusive endpoints), population filters (test accounts, internal users, refunds, cancelled records), source tables (are they built for different purposes, at different grain?), and refresh timing. Reconcile to a single agreed figure by walking the difference: start from one number and add each discrepancy until you land on the other.

Then the part that marks out seniority: "Once we agreed the definition I would get it written into a shared, documented model so the discrepancy cannot recur, and I would frame it as neither team being wrong, because that is how you keep both of them cooperating."

When DISTINCT is a warning sign

"Your query returns duplicates so you added DISTINCT. What would you check first?"

Reaching for DISTINCT to make duplicates disappear is one of the most common ways analysts hide a real bug. Duplicates in a result set usually mean one of two things: the grain is finer than you assumed, or a join has fanned out because the key was not unique on one side.

Lesson

The WHERE clause

💡 Why this module matters: filtering is the most common thing you will ever do in SQL. "Only 2025", "only the UK", "only respondents who completed", "only rows where the value isn't missing". WHERE is filter(), and mastering its operators means you can answer most ad-hoc questions with one query.

WHERE sits between FROM and everything else, and keeps only rows where the condition is true:

SELECT name, circuit, race_date
FROM races
WHERE season = 2025;

The comparison operators: =, != (or <>), >, <, >=, <=. Text values go in single quotes: WHERE nationality = 'GBR'.

🚨 The one that bites R users: SQL uses a single = for comparison, not ==. And it uses single quotes for text, where R lets you use either. Write WHERE code == 'VER' and some databases will forgive you, but many won't.

Lesson

Combining and pattern-matching

-- AND / OR combine conditions (brackets clarify precedence)
WHERE season = 2025 AND round <= 3

-- IN matches against a list
WHERE code IN ('VER', 'NOR', 'LEC')

-- BETWEEN is an inclusive range
WHERE points BETWEEN 10 AND 18

-- LIKE pattern-matches text. % = any characters, _ = one character
WHERE surname LIKE 'H%'   -- starts with H

🧠 The R translation: IN is %in%, AND is &, OR is |, and LIKE 'H%' is roughly str_starts(surname, "H"). Your filter(region %in% c("UK","US") & wave > 3) becomes WHERE region IN ('UK','US') AND wave > 3.

Lesson

NULL: the database's NA

SQL's missing value is NULL, and it behaves like R's NA: it isn't equal to anything, not even itself. WHERE position = NULL matches nothing, silently. You must use the special operators:

-- In our data, a retired car (DNF) has position = NULL.
-- Find all DNFs:
SELECT race_id, driver_id, status
FROM results
WHERE position IS NULL;

-- Find all classified finishers:
WHERE position IS NOT NULL

This mirrors R exactly: you write is.na(x), never x == NA. Same trap, same solution.

Interview note
💡 Interview note

Three-valued logic and the NULL trap

"Why does WHERE status != 'cancelled' not return rows where status is NULL?"

This is asked constantly because it catches out people who have used SQL for years. SQL has three truth values: true, false, and unknown. Any comparison with NULL yields unknown, and WHERE keeps only rows that evaluate to true. So a NULL status is neither equal to nor unequal to 'cancelled': the test returns unknown and the row is dropped.

The complete answer: "Comparisons against NULL return unknown rather than true or false, and WHERE only keeps true. I would write WHERE status != 'cancelled' OR status IS NULL, or use COALESCE if a default makes sense."

If you want to go further, mention that this is also why NOT IN with a subquery containing a single NULL returns nothing at all, which is a genuinely nasty production bug and the reason experienced analysts prefer NOT EXISTS.

Lesson

ORDER BY

💡 Why this module matters: half of all analytical questions are secretly top-N questions. Fastest pit stops, biggest markets, worst-performing SKUs, most recent orders. ORDER BY plus LIMIT is the two-line pattern that answers all of them.

SELECT race_id, driver_id, duration_s
FROM pit_stops
ORDER BY duration_s ASC    -- ascending is the default
LIMIT 5;                    -- ...so this is "the 5 fastest stops"
  • DESC flips to descending: biggest first. This is your arrange(desc(x)).
  • Sort by several columns by listing them: ORDER BY season DESC, round ASC. Ties on the first column are broken by the second.
  • ORDER BY goes near the end of a query, after WHERE, and LIMIT always comes last.

⚠️ No ORDER BY means no promised order. Without it, the database returns rows in whatever order is convenient for it. If the order of your output matters, even slightly, say so explicitly. "It came out sorted last time" is not a guarantee.

Top-N per group is not a LIMIT question

"Give me the top three products by revenue in each category."

A near-universal follow-up to any basic sorting question, and the trap is that LIMIT 3 gives you three rows overall rather than three per category. There is no way to express "per group" with LIMIT alone.

Aggregation: GROUP BY and HAVING

The pivot table, expressed in words. This is where SQL starts to feel powerful, and where the difference between filtering rows and filtering groups becomes essential.

⏱️ 40 min 🎯 5 challenges 🏆 1 badge

By the end of this module you will be able to

Lesson

Collapse rows into groups

💡 Why this module matters: this is where SQL becomes an analysis tool rather than a retrieval tool. A championship standings table, a crosstab, an Excel pivot table, your group_by() %>% summarise() pipelines: they are all the same idea, and in SQL that idea is GROUP BY. This is the module interviewers probe hardest.

GROUP BY collapses many rows into one row per unique value, and aggregate functions do the maths within each group: COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX.

-- The drivers' championship is literally a GROUP BY:
SELECT driver_id,
       SUM(points) AS total_points,
       COUNT(*) AS races_entered
FROM results
GROUP BY driver_id
ORDER BY total_points DESC;

The iron rule: every column in SELECT must either be aggregated or appear in GROUP BY. If it's neither, the database has no way to know which row's value to display, and will (rightly) refuse.

🧠 The R translation: results %>% group_by(driver_id) %>% summarise(total_points = sum(points), races_entered = n()). The mapping is one-to-one: GROUP BY is group_by(), the aggregates are what goes inside summarise(), and COUNT(*) is n().

Lesson

COUNT's three faces, and NULLs

COUNT(*)                  -- all rows in the group, full stop
COUNT(position)           -- rows where position is NOT NULL
COUNT(DISTINCT driver_id) -- unique non-NULL values

Because DNFs have position = NULL, COUNT(position) quietly counts only finishers, a genuinely useful trick. The same applies to AVG: NULLs are ignored, not treated as zero, which is usually what you want and occasionally exactly what you don't.

HAVING: filtering the groups themselves

WHERE filters rows before grouping. HAVING filters groups after aggregation. You need HAVING the moment your condition involves an aggregate:

-- Drivers with 2 or more DNFs:
SELECT driver_id, COUNT(*) AS dnfs
FROM results
WHERE position IS NULL      -- row filter: keep only DNF rows
GROUP BY driver_id
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 2;        -- group filter: keep only repeat offenders

🧠 The R translation: HAVING is simply filter() placed after summarise() in a pipe. dplyr doesn't need a separate word for it because the pipe makes order explicit; SQL does because everything lives in one statement.

The three COUNTs

"What is the difference between COUNT(*), COUNT(column) and COUNT(DISTINCT column)?"

One of the most reliably asked SQL questions in existence, because the answer reveals whether you understand NULL handling.

COUNT(*) counts rows, full stop, including rows where every column is NULL. COUNT(column) counts rows where that column is not NULL, so it is simultaneously a count and a completeness check. COUNT(DISTINCT column) counts distinct non-NULL values.

The practical framing that lands well: "I use the gap between COUNT(*) and COUNT(column) as a quick null check, and COUNT(DISTINCT id) whenever the grain of the table is finer than the thing I am counting."

Worth adding: AVG also ignores NULLs, so the average of a column with missing values is computed over the non-missing rows only. If you wanted missing to count as zero, AVG gives you a different and higher number than you expected, silently.

SELECT d.surname, r.points
FROM results r                        -- "r" is a table alias
JOIN drivers d                        -- bring in a second table
  ON r.driver_id = d.driver_id        -- ...matched on this column
WHERE r.race_id = 1;

Three moving parts: the second table, the ON condition saying which columns must match, and table aliases (r, d) so you can say which table each column comes from. Once two tables are joined, you can join a third onto the result, and a fourth, chaining as far as you need.

What a join does to your row count

"You LEFT JOIN orders to customers and end up with more rows than you started with. What happened?"

The expected answer: a LEFT JOIN guarantees you keep every left row at least once, not exactly once. If the right table has several rows matching the same key, the left row is duplicated once per match. Extra rows mean the join key is not unique on the right.

Say what you would do: "I would check uniqueness on the right with a group by on the join key having count greater than one. If duplicates are legitimate, I would aggregate the right table to one row per key before joining."

The complementary case is worth naming too, because it is the more dangerous one: an INNER JOIN silently drops left rows that have no match, so your total quietly falls and nothing warns you. That is why LEFT JOIN is the safer default when you are decorating data with lookups, and why comparing row counts before and after every join is a habit worth having.

What to take away

  • INNER JOIN keeps only matches, so it filters as well as joins, which is easy to forget
  • Joining on a non-unique key multiplies rows, and that fan-out inflates every subsequent SUM

Subqueries: a query inside a query

💡 Why this module matters: real analytical questions rarely fit in one step. "Which pit stops beat the average?" needs the average first. In R you'd make an intermediate object and carry on; SQL's equivalents are subqueries and, much more readably, CTEs. Writing clean CTEs is what makes your SQL look senior.

-- Pit stops faster than the overall average:
SELECT driver_id, duration_s
FROM pit_stops
WHERE duration_s < (SELECT AVG(duration_s) FROM pit_stops);

The bracketed query runs first and collapses to a single number, which the outer WHERE then compares against. Subqueries can also return lists (used with IN) or whole tables (used in FROM). They're powerful but nest badly: three layers deep and nobody, including future you, can read it.

Lesson

CTEs: named steps with WITH

A Common Table Expression gives a sub-result a name, exactly like assigning an intermediate dataframe in R before the next pipe:

-- The 2025 drivers' championship:
WITH standings AS (
  SELECT r.driver_id, SUM(r.points) AS pts
  FROM results r
  JOIN races ra ON r.race_id = ra.race_id
  WHERE ra.season = 2025
  GROUP BY r.driver_id
)
SELECT d.code, s.pts
FROM standings s
JOIN drivers d ON s.driver_id = d.driver_id
ORDER BY s.pts DESC
LIMIT 10;

🧠 The R translation: a CTE is standings <- results %>% ... %>% summarise(...) followed by a second pipeline that uses standings. Multiple CTEs chain with commas: WITH a AS (...), b AS (...), and later CTEs can reference earlier ones, just like intermediate objects in a script.

⚠️ Style guidance you'll thank yourself for: when a query grows past one aggregation or one join, reach for a CTE rather than nesting subqueries. Name CTEs after what they contain (standings, dnf_counts), not t1, t2. Reviewers, interviewers and future-you all read CTE-style SQL far faster.

CTE or subquery, and why it matters

"When would you use a CTE rather than a subquery?"

Three reasons, and giving all three shows you have written real queries rather than just read about them.

Readability: a CTE gives an intermediate step a name, so a 60 line query becomes a sequence of understandable stages instead of nested brackets read inside out. Window functions and execution order

Window functions are the single largest step up in SQL capability after joins, and they are the most common thing candidates cannot do in a technical screen. They also force you to understand the order a query really runs in, which explains half the error messages you have ever seen.

Lesson

The problem GROUP BY cannot solve

GROUP BY collapses. Twenty rows become one row per group, and the detail is gone. That is usually what you want, and sometimes it is precisely what you cannot afford.

Consider a genuinely common request: list every race result, and next to each one show that driver's season total, and what percentage of their season that race represented. You need the detail row and the aggregate side by side. GROUP BY cannot do it, because the aggregate destroys the detail.

The usual workaround is to aggregate separately and join back:

-- The old way: aggregate, then join back to the detail
WITH totals AS (
  SELECT driver_id, SUM(points) AS season_pts
  FROM results GROUP BY driver_id
)
SELECT r.race_id, r.driver_id, r.points, t.season_pts
FROM results r
JOIN totals t ON r.driver_id = t.driver_id;

It works. It is also verbose, and it needs a second pass over the table for every aggregate you want. A window function does the same thing in one clause:

-- The window way: aggregate alongside the detail, one pass
SELECT race_id, driver_id, points,
       SUM(points) OVER (PARTITION BY driver_id) AS season_pts
FROM results;

The one sentence definition worth memorising: a window function computes a value across a set of rows related to the current row, without collapsing those rows. Every row survives, and each one gains a column that knows something about its neighbours.

Lesson

Anatomy of the OVER clause

Everything sits inside OVER (...), which has three optional parts. Each does something specific.

SUM(points) OVER (
    PARTITION BY driver_id   -- reset the calculation per driver
    ORDER BY round           -- process rows in this order
    ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW   -- the frame
)
  • PARTITION BY divides the rows into independent groups. It is GROUP BY's idea without the collapsing. Omit it and the whole result set is one window.
  • ORDER BY sequences rows inside each partition. Required by anything positional, such as ROW_NUMBER or LAG.
  • The frame defines which rows around the current one are included.

Here is the part that catches people, and it is worth knowing precisely. Adding ORDER BY to an aggregate window silently changes the default frame:

WrittenDefault frameResult
SUM(x) OVER (PARTITION BY d)The whole partitionThe same grand total on every row
SUM(x) OVER (PARTITION BY d ORDER BY r)Start of partition to current rowA running total

So ORDER BY inside a window is not a cosmetic sorting instruction. It converts a total into a cumulative total. If your running total comes out as the same number repeated, you left out ORDER BY. If your grand total comes out ascending, you put one in by accident.

The ROWS versus RANGE distinction: ROWS counts physical rows, RANGE groups by value, so tied values in the ORDER BY column are all included together. With duplicate dates, RANGE (which is the default) gives every tied row the same running total, which is usually not what you meant. Write ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW explicitly when you want a true row-by-row cumulative sum.

The ranking family, and why the choice matters

Three functions that look interchangeable and behave differently the moment there is a tie. Here is real output from the course database, 2025 season totals:

       ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY t.pts DESC) AS rownum,

RIC      1       18    18      16
ZHO      1       19    18      16

Read the three columns across the ties and the difference is unmistakable:

  • ROW_NUMBER always gives distinct consecutive integers. Ties are broken arbitrarily, so ALB gets 14 and MAG gets 15 for no defensible reason. Never use it to express rank; use it to pick exactly one row per group.
  • RANK gives ties the same rank and then skips: 14, 14, 16. This is competition ranking, the "joint 14th" you see in sport.
  • DENSE_RANK gives ties the same rank and does not skip: 14, 14, 15. Use it when you want "the top 3 distinct values" rather than "the top 3 rows".

The pattern that earns its keep: ROW_NUMBER with PARTITION BY is how you pick one row per group. The most recent order per customer, the cheapest supplier per part, the fastest stop per race. Number the rows within each partition, then filter to = 1. It also deduplicates: partition by whatever should be unique, order by a tiebreaker such as most recently updated, and keep row 1.

Looking backwards and forwards: LAG and LEAD

LAG fetches a value from an earlier row in the partition; LEAD from a later one. This is how period-on-period comparison gets done in SQL, and it removes an enormous amount of awkward self-joining.

SELECT round, points,
       LAG(points) OVER (ORDER BY round) AS prev_points,
       points - LAG(points) OVER (ORDER BY round) AS change
FROM results -- filtered to one driver

round  points  prev_points  change
    1      15         NULL    NULL
    2      10           15      -5
    3       8           10      -2
    4      12            8       4
    5      25           12      13

Two things to note. The first row's LAG is NULL, because there is nothing before it; supply a third argument for a default, as in LAG(points, 1, 0). And LAG always needs ORDER BY, since "the previous row" is meaningless without a defined sequence.

The combination you will reach for constantly, month-on-month change per group:

LAG(revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY market ORDER BY month)

Partition so each market's series is independent, order so "previous" means the previous month within that market. Get the partition wrong and one market's January silently compares against another market's December.

The R translation: LAG and LEAD are dplyr's lag() and lead(), and PARTITION BY is group_by() without a following summarise(). If you have written df %>% group_by(market) %>% arrange(month) %>% mutate(change = revenue - lag(revenue)), you have already used a window function; SQL just makes the window explicit rather than implied by the grouping context.

The order a query actually runs in

You write a query in one order. The database evaluates it in another. Learning the second order explains a whole family of error messages that otherwise look arbitrary.

#StepWhat exists by now
1FROM and JOINThe combined row set
2WHEREIndividual rows filtered
3GROUP BYRows collapsed into groups
4Aggregate functionsGroup-level values
5HAVINGGroups filtered
6Window functionsWindow columns added
7SELECTOutput columns and aliases
8DISTINCTDuplicate output rows removed
9ORDER BYResult sorted
10LIMITResult truncated

Three consequences fall straight out of that table, and they are the answers to questions you will be asked.

You cannot use a SELECT alias in WHERE. WHERE runs at step 2, the alias is created at step 7. It does not exist yet. You can use it in ORDER BY, because that runs at step 9, after SELECT. That asymmetry is not a quirk, it is the execution order made visible.

WHERE filters rows, HAVING filters groups. WHERE runs before grouping, so it cannot see an aggregate. HAVING runs after, so it can. Filter as much as possible in WHERE, because doing so before aggregation is cheaper.

You cannot filter on a window function in WHERE. Windows are computed at step 6, after WHERE at step 2. So WHERE ROW_NUMBER() OVER (...) = 1 is an error in every dialect. The window has to be computed in a subquery or CTE, and filtered in the outer query. That is precisely why the fastest-stop-per-race challenge needed a CTE, rather than the author being fussy about style.

-- Illegal: the window does not exist when WHERE runs
SELECT race_id, driver_id
FROM pit_stops
WHERE ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY race_id ORDER BY duration_s) = 1;

-- Legal: compute in a CTE, filter outside it
WITH ranked AS (
  SELECT race_id, driver_id,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY race_id ORDER BY duration_s) AS rn
  FROM pit_stops
)
SELECT race_id, driver_id FROM ranked WHERE rn = 1;

Mutability, and the copy that is not a copy

Why this matters in analysis work: the same distinction governs pandas, where a DataFrame slice may be a view onto the original rather than an independent copy. The SettingWithCopyWarning you will meet later is exactly this problem in a bigger costume.

Indentation IS the syntax

What's new is the packaging: Python uses indentation instead of curly braces, def instead of function(), and has one construct R lacks, the list comprehension, which will quietly become your favourite thing in the language.

In R, braces define blocks and indentation is decoration. In Python, indentation is the block. A colon opens the block, and everything indented beneath it belongs to it:

def classify_finish(pos):
    if pos == 1:
        return "Win"
    elif pos <= 3:          # "elif", one word, not "else if"
        return "Podium"
    elif pos <= 10:
        return "Points"
    else:
        return "No reward"

Note also: def replaces function(), and return is compulsory. A Python function with no return gives back None, unlike R's helpful last-expression rule. Default arguments work as you'd hope: def points(pos, sprint=False):.

The mutable default argument

"What is wrong with def add_item(item, basket=[])?"

The best-known Python gotcha, and it appears in interviews precisely because the explanation requires understanding when code runs.

Default arguments are evaluated once, when the function is defined, not each time it is called. So every call that omits basket shares the same list, and items accumulate across calls that were supposed to be independent.

The answer: "Mutable defaults are created once at definition time and shared between calls. The fix is def add_item(item, basket=None) and then if basket is None: basket = [] inside the function."

If you want to demonstrate the underlying principle rather than the memorised trick, add that the same reasoning explains why None is compared with is rather than ==: is tests identity, and there is only ever one None object.

Boolean masks: filter() the pandas way

💡 Why this module matters: this is filter() and select(), the two verbs you use most in R, translated. The pandas versions are slightly stranger (boolean masks, .loc), but they're also the third telling of the same story you've now heard in dplyr and in SQL's WHERE. Third time through, it sticks.

The core move: a comparison on a column produces a Series of True/False, and putting that inside square brackets keeps the True rows:

# One condition:
wins = results[results["position"] == 1]

# Multiple conditions: & and |, each condition in brackets
mclaren_wins = results[(results["position"] == 1) &
                       (results["team"] == "McLaren")]

# List membership and ranges:
top3 = results[results["code"].isin(["VER", "NOR", "LEC"])]
mid  = results[results["points"].between(4, 12)]

🚨 Two traps for R users: pandas needs & and | (never and/or), and each condition must be wrapped in its own brackets, because & binds tighter than ==. Forgetting the brackets gives a baffling error; forgetting and writing and gives an even more baffling one. Both take two seconds to fix once you know to look.

Lesson

loc and iloc: labels vs positions

# .loc = by label/condition, rows, then columns:
results.loc[results["points"] > 15, ["code", "points"]]

# .iloc = by integer position (zero-based, end excluded):
results.iloc[0:5, 0:3]   # first 5 rows, first 3 columns

.loc[rows, columns] is the full-strength version of filtering: condition on the left of the comma, column list on the right, so it's filter() and select() in one call. .iloc is for "give me rows 10 to 20" positional work. When modifying data (next modules), .loc is also the safe way to assign, avoiding pandas' famous SettingWithCopyWarning.

🧠 The R translation: results.loc[results["points"] > 15, ["code","points"]] is results %>% filter(points > 15) %>% select(code, points). And .iloc[0:5] is df[1:5, ], minus one from everything, exclude the end.

groupby and agg

💡 Why this module matters: this is the third and final telling of split-apply-combine. You know it as group_by() %>% summarise(), you've now written it as GROUP BY in SQL, and in pandas it's .groupby(). When the same idea appears in all three tools, that's the tool-makers telling you what data analysis actually is.

# One statistic:
results.groupby("team")["points"].sum()

# Several statistics with named outputs, this is summarise():
standings = (
    results
    .groupby("code")
    .agg(
        total_pts = ("points", "sum"),
        races     = ("race_id", "count"),
        best      = ("position", "min")
    )
    .reset_index()
    .sort_values("total_pts", ascending=False)
)

Read the agg lines as new_name = ("column", "statistic"). Wrapping the chain in brackets lets you put each method on its own line, which is the accepted pandas equivalent of a piped dplyr block, and reads almost identically.

🚨 The reset_index() gotcha: groupby moves your grouping column into the DataFrame's index (row labels), which makes subsequent filtering and merging awkward. .reset_index() turns it back into an ordinary column. R has no equivalent concept, so the rule of thumb while you're learning: end every groupby chain with .reset_index() and life stays simple.

Interview note

agg, transform, apply

"What is the difference between agg, transform and apply on a groupby?"

This is the question that separates people who use pandas from people who know it, and the distinction is about the shape of what comes back.

agg returns one row per group: it reduces. transform returns one row per original row, broadcasting the group result back onto every member, so you can put each row's group total beside it. apply is the flexible fallback that can return either, and it is the slowest because it hands each group to a Python function.

The answer that lands: "agg reduces to one row per group, transform preserves the original shape, and apply does anything but pays for the flexibility. If I need group totals alongside detail rows I use transform, which is the direct equivalent of a SQL window function."

Connecting transform to SUM() OVER (PARTITION BY ...) out loud is a strong move, because it shows you see the same idea across two languages rather than memorising two APIs.

merge: the join family in one function

💡 Why this module matters: joins, third telling. left_join() in R, LEFT JOIN in SQL, and now pd.merge(). Plus pandas' answer to pivot_longer()/pivot_wider(), which you'll need every time a chart or a model wants data in a different shape than it arrived in.

# Put driver names on results:
named = pd.merge(results, drivers,
                 on="driver_id",     # the join key
                 how="left")          # left / inner / right / outer

# Different key names on each side:
named = pd.merge(results, drivers,
                 left_on="driver_id", right_on="id", how="left")

# Stacking files (bind_rows / UNION ALL):
both_seasons = pd.concat([res_2024, res_2025], ignore_index=True)

One function, and the how= argument picks the join type, with "outer" playing the role of FULL OUTER JOIN. Everything you learned about join safety in the SQL module applies verbatim: check row counts before and after, know which side is "left".

🧠 A trick R doesn't have: pd.merge(..., indicator=True) adds a _merge column saying whether each row matched (both, left_only, right_only). Run value_counts() on it and you have an instant join-quality report. It's the built-in version of the anti-join checks you do by hand in R.

Performance, memory and scale

The moment your data outgrows a few thousand rows, the difference between code that runs in seconds and code that runs in twenty minutes is a handful of habits. This module covers those habits, plus the harder judgement of knowing when pandas is the wrong tool entirely.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Explain why vectorised operations beat loops, and rank the approaches by speed
  • Cut memory use with appropriate dtypes and the category type
  • Understand copies, views and why SettingWithCopyWarning appears
  • Recognise when to push work to SQL or reach for a different tool
Lesson

Why loops are slow, and vectors are not

pandas is built on NumPy, which stores each column as a single contiguous block of memory of one fixed type. Operations on that block are executed in compiled C, looping at machine speed over memory that sits together in the cache.

When you write a Python for loop over rows, you give all of that up. Every iteration goes back into the Python interpreter, boxes each value into a Python object, and touches memory scattered across rows rather than packed along columns. The arithmetic is identical; the overhead around it is not.

# Four ways to do the same thing, slowest to fastest

# 1. iterrows: constructs a Series per row. Avoid.
total = []
for i, row in df.iterrows():
    total.append(row["price"] * row["qty"])

# 2. apply with axis=1: still a Python call per row
df["total"] = df.apply(lambda r: r["price"] * r["qty"], axis=1)

# 3. itertuples: much faster than iterrows if you must iterate
total = [t.price * t.qty for t in df.itertuples()]

# 4. vectorised: one C-level operation over two columns
df["total"] = df["price"] * df["qty"]

On a million rows, the gap between the first and last of these is typically two to three orders of magnitude. The last one is also the shortest and the easiest to read, which is unusual and welcome.

The R parallel is exact. You already know not to loop over rows in R, because R's vectorised operations drop into C for the same reasons. df$total <- df$price * df$qty is the identical instinct. If you find yourself writing iterrows, ask what the R one-liner would have been.

Lesson

Replacing the loops you thought you needed

Most row loops exist because someone did not know the vectorised equivalent. These four cover the majority of cases.

Conditional logic: np.where for two outcomes, np.select for several.

import numpy as np

# two-way
df["band"] = np.where(df["value"] > 100, "high", "low")

# multi-way, first match wins, like dplyr::case_when
conds = [df["pos"] == 1, df["pos"] <= 3, df["pos"] <= 10]
labels = ["Win", "Podium", "Points"]
df["outcome"] = np.select(conds, labels, default="No reward")

Lookups and recoding: map with a dict beats a loop, and merge beats both for anything larger.

df["team_full"] = df["team_code"].map({"RBR": "Red Bull", "MCL": "McLaren"})

Text operations: the .str accessor is vectorised.

df["code"] = df["code"].str.strip().str.upper()
df["is_gp"] = df["name"].str.contains("Grand Prix", na=False)

Group-level calculations without collapsing: transform is the direct equivalent of a SQL window function, and it is the pandas feature most often reimplemented badly as a loop.

# each row gains its group's total, rows preserved
df["team_total"] = df.groupby("team")["points"].transform("sum")
df["share"]      = df["points"] / df["team_total"]

agg versus transform: agg returns one row per group, transform returns one row per original row. If you have ever written a groupby, then merged the result back to get the totals alongside the detail, transform was the one-line version. It is the same relationship as GROUP BY against SUM() OVER (PARTITION BY ...) in SQL.

Memory: dtypes are a performance decision

pandas defaults are generous rather than efficient. A column of small integers arrives as int64, eight bytes per value whether you need them or not, and text arrives as object, which stores a pointer to a separate Python string for every single row.

df.info(memory_usage="deep")   # deep = include object contents

# typical wins
df["year"]   = df["year"].astype("int16")      # 8 bytes -> 2
df["region"] = df["region"].astype("category")  # often 10x or better

The category dtype is the big one and it should feel familiar: it stores the distinct values once and holds small integer codes per row. That is precisely what a factor is in R, and what labelled survey data does. On a column of 5 million rows containing twelve region names, the saving is dramatic.

Two further benefits: groupby on a categorical is faster, and an ordered categorical carries its own sort order, so charts and tables come out in scale order rather than alphabetically. That last point solves a problem you have hit in every piece of survey reporting you have ever done.

Where category hurts: adding a value not already in the categories raises an error or produces NaN, and concatenating frames with different category sets can silently fall back to object. Use it for genuinely closed sets (region, brand, response option), not for high-cardinality fields such as free text, where it uses more memory rather than less.

Also worth knowing: read the types in at load time rather than converting afterwards, since conversion needs both versions in memory at once.

df = pd.read_csv("big.csv",
                 usecols=["date", "region", "value"],   # read only what you need
                 dtype={"region": "category"},
                 parse_dates=["date"])

Copies, views and the warning everyone ignores

SettingWithCopyWarning is the most-ignored message in pandas and the one most likely to cost you a silent data error.

The cause: some pandas operations return a view onto the original data, others return a copy, and which you get is not always predictable. If you filter and then assign, you may be writing to a temporary copy that is discarded, leaving the original untouched.

# Risky: chained indexing. Did this write to df, or to a copy?
df[df["season"] == 2025]["flag"] = True      # warning, often no effect

# Correct: one .loc call does the selection and the assignment together
df.loc[df["season"] == 2025, "flag"] = True

# Working on a subset deliberately? Take an explicit copy.
recent = df[df["season"] == 2025].copy()
recent["flag"] = True          # unambiguous, no warning

Two rules remove the whole problem. Never chain square brackets for assignment; use a single .loc[rows, cols]. And call .copy() whenever you intend a subset to be an independent object.

Where this is heading: pandas is moving to copy-on-write semantics, which makes the behaviour predictable (operations behave as if they always copy, with the copy deferred for performance) and removes this class of bug. It became the default in pandas 3.0. The two rules above are correct under both regimes, so they are worth keeping regardless of version.

Readable pipelines with method chaining

Coming from dplyr, you will miss the pipe. pandas has an equivalent idiom: wrap the whole expression in brackets and chain, one operation per line.

standings = (
    results
    .query("season == 2025")
    .groupby("code", as_index=False)
    .agg(total_pts=("points", "sum"),
         races=("race_id", "count"))
    .assign(avg_pts=lambda d: d["total_pts"] / d["races"])
    .sort_values("total_pts", ascending=False)
    .reset_index(drop=True)
)

The mapping from dplyr is almost one to one: query is filter, assign is mutate, agg is summarise, sort_values is arrange. The lambda d: inside assign is how you refer to the frame as it exists at that point in the chain, which is what makes referring to total_pts possible before it exists anywhere else.

Beyond aesthetics, chaining avoids creating a dozen intermediate variables, each holding a full copy of your data, and it removes the classic bug of accidentally using df2 when you meant df3. Use .pipe(your_function) to insert a custom step without breaking the chain.

Lesson

When pandas is the wrong answer

Knowing the limits of your tool is a senior trait, and the honest limits are these.

pandas is single-threaded and in-memory. The working rule of thumb is that you want roughly five to ten times your data size in RAM, because intermediate operations copy. A 4GB CSV on a 16GB laptop is uncomfortable, and 40GB is simply out of scope.

SituationBetter move
Aggregating a huge table down to a small resultDo it in SQL. Let the warehouse aggregate and bring back the summary
Larger than memory, single machinePolars (multi-threaded, lazy) or DuckDB (SQL over files, out of core)
Genuinely distributed scaleSpark, or the warehouse itself
Repeated production transformationPush it upstream into the modelled layer, so everyone gets it
Wide file, few columns neededusecols at read time, or a columnar format such as Parquet

The most useful habit here costs nothing: filter and aggregate as early as possible, ideally in the query. Pulling 10 million rows into pandas to compute twelve monthly totals wastes both memory and time, and the warehouse is better at it than your laptop. A good analyst's SQL does the heavy lifting; pandas handles the shaping, the fiddly cleaning and the parts SQL is awkward at.

Parquet deserves a mention because you will meet it constantly. It is columnar, compressed and it stores the schema, so reading three columns from a Parquet file genuinely reads three columns, types survive the round trip, and files are a fraction of CSV size. If you control the format, choose it.
